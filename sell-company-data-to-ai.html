How to sell your company's data to AI companies.

Five steps, in the order they actually happen. Most owners get stuck on the first one.

Step one

Start by proving you own the record.

This is where most deals die, and it dies quietly because owners assume the answer is yes.

A company holds two kinds of material. The first is its own operating record, which it owns outright. The second is material held on behalf of somebody else, usually a client under a master agreement that assigns ownership to them. The second kind cannot be licensed no matter how valuable it looks.

Sort this before anything else. A buyer will ask for a clean provenance chain, and a seller who cannot produce one has no asset to sell.

Step two

Find what is actually in there.

Owners routinely misjudge their own record in both directions.

The systems that turn out to matter are rarely the ones people name first. Support tickets, internal threads, quote revisions, field notes, and call recordings tend to carry more of the reasoning than the CRM or the accounting export does.

The question to answer is not how many gigabytes exist. It is how many years of judgment are visible inside them.

Step three

Price it against live demand.

There is no public price list for this asset, and the comparables that circulate publicly come from large publishers licensing catalogs. They are the wrong reference point for an operating business.

Pricing here means knowing what buyers are currently paying for a record of this shape, depth, and cleanliness. That knowledge sits with whoever is running deals continuously, which is the practical argument for representation over a solo approach.

Step four

Paper a license, not a sale.

The instinct is to sell the data. The better structure is to license it.

A license grants defined rights over defined material for a defined period. The company keeps ownership, keeps operating on its own records, and can license again later. A sale ends all of that permanently in exchange for a single payment.

Scope and exclusivity are the two terms that move price the most, and they are the two most often given away by a seller negotiating alone.

Step five

Anonymize before delivery.

Identifying material is stripped as part of preparing the asset, before anything leaves the company's systems. This protects the seller and it is also what makes the asset saleable, since a buyer with compliance obligations will not touch a record that carries personal information.

Questions

Common questions about selling to AI companies.

How do I sell my company's data to AI companies?

Establish that you own the records, take an inventory of what the operating history actually contains, price it against live buyer demand, paper a bounded license, then anonymize and deliver. Companies that go direct handle all five themselves. Companies that use sell-side representation hand off the pricing, the buyer relationships, and the negotiation.

Do I need to approach the AI labs myself?

No. A represented seller takes no meetings with buyers. The buyer relationships and the process sit with the representative, which is the arrangement Polyshares runs.

What do AI companies want from an ordinary business?

The working record rather than the polished output. Threads, tickets, drafts, revisions, and correspondence that show a problem being worked through. Finished reports carry the answer without the reasoning that produced it.

How long does the process take?

At Polyshares, terms are papered within a week of the first conversation and money reaches the company account in eight to twelve weeks, on the buyer closing. A company running the process alone should expect materially longer, because most of the elapsed time is spent finding and qualifying buyers.

What does it cost to start?

Nothing, under a proceeds-based fee. Polyshares is paid out of the deal, so a company that never sells owes nothing.
